Renewable Energy Research

What is Renewable Energy?

Renewable energy is energy collected from resources that are naturally replenished on a human timescale — sunlight, wind, rain, tides and geothermal heat. Unlike fossil fuels, which took millions of years to form, these sources are continuously available and produce little to no greenhouse gas emissions during operation.

The burning of fossil fuels — coal, oil and gas — accounts for approximately 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Transitioning to renewables is therefore the single largest lever available to limit warming to 1.5°C, as set out in the Paris Agreement.

Key Facts

UN SDG 13 calls for affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy access for all by 2030. Renewables are the primary pathway to achieving this goal while simultaneously tackling climate change.

Solar Power

Solar power converts energy from the Sun into electricity using two main technologies. Photovoltaic (PV) panels use the photoelectric effect to generate electricity directly from sunlight, while Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) uses mirrors to focus heat onto a steam turbine.

How Photovoltaic Cells Work

A PV cell is made of semiconductor material — typically silicon. When photons from sunlight strike the cell, electrons are knocked loose, creating an electric current. Multiple cells form a module (panel), and arrays of panels generate electricity at scale.

Fig 2. Rooftop solar PV installations allow homes and businesses to generate their own clean electricity.

Advantages and Challenges

  • Zero emissions during operation — no air or noise pollution.
  • Highly scalable — from pocket devices to grid-scale farms.
  • Rapidly falling costs — below $0.05/kWh in many regions.
  • Intermittency — only generates during daylight; needs battery storage.
  • Land use — utility-scale farms require significant space and planning.

Key Facts

In 2023, solar PV accounted for two-thirds of all new renewable capacity additions globally. It is now the cheapest source of new electricity generation in history (IEA, 2023).

Wind Energy

Wind energy converts the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity via turbines. Modern wind turbines are engineering marvels — a single large offshore turbine can generate enough power to supply over 1,000 homes for an entire year.

Fig 3. Onshore wind turbines generating clean electricity — farmland between turbines can still be used for crops and grazing.

Onshore vs. Offshore Wind

  • Onshore wind is currently the cheapest form of new electricity generation in most countries, with turbines ranging from small farm-scale machines to 5 MW+ commercial wind farms.
  • Offshore wind captures stronger, more consistent ocean winds. Though costlier to build, turbines can be larger and output is less variable. The UK, Denmark and China lead globally.
  • Global wind capacity exceeded 900 GW in 2023, covering approximately 7% of global electricity demand.
  • Lifecycle emissions of wind are just 7–15 gCO²/kWh, versus 820 gCO²/kWh for coal power.

Key Facts

Turbine heights now exceed 300 metres for the largest offshore models. Wind energy's land footprint is minimal — the agricultural land around turbines remains fully usable for farming.

Hydropower

Hydropower is the world's largest source of renewable electricity, supplying approximately 16% of global electricity. It converts the potential energy of water at height into electricity as water flows through turbines in a dam or run-of-river system.

Fig 4. A large hydroelectric dam and reservoir — hydropower remains the most established renewable electricity technology worldwide.

Types of Hydropower

  • Large-scale (impoundment) — reservoir dams such as the Three Gorges Dam in China (22.5 GW capacity).
  • Run-of-river — diverts part of a river's flow with minimal storage and reduced ecological disruption.
  • Pumped-storage — acts as a giant battery; water is pumped uphill using surplus electricity and released during peak demand.
  • Small-scale (<10 MW) — highly suitable for remote communities; Nepal has enormous untapped potential in this area.

Key Facts

Nepal has an estimated hydropower potential of 83,000 MW — one of the largest in Asia relative to its size — but only around 2,000 MW is currently developed. Responsible exploitation could transform Nepal into a major regional clean energy exporter.

Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy taps the vast heat stored within the Earth — produced by the decay of radioactive elements and residual heat from the planet's formation. It provides consistent, 24/7 baseload power with very low emissions and an extremely small land footprint.

Fig 3. A geothermal power plant in Iceland — the country generates over 30% of its electricity from geothermal sources.

How It Works

Hot water or steam is extracted from underground reservoirs through production wells. Steam drives a turbine generator; cooled fluid is re-injected to maintain reservoir pressure — making geothermal a near-zero-waste energy cycle.

Iceland generates over 30% of its electricity and nearly 90% of its heating from geothermal sources, making it one of the cleanest energy systems in the world.

Global Progress Towards SDG 13

The United Nations reviews SDG 13 progress annually. While remarkable strides have been made — particularly in cost reduction and electricity access — the world is not yet on track to meet the 2030 targets.

Key Milestones

2015 — Paris Agreement

195 countries commit to limiting warming to 1.5°C; renewables are central to all national climate pledges.

2017 — Solar cheaper than coal

New solar PV becomes cheaper than new coal power in most countries for the first time (IRENA).

2020 — UK goes coal-free

The United Kingdom goes 67 consecutive days without coal power — the longest since the Industrial Revolution.

2022 — EU REPowerEU

Europe accelerates renewables rollout targeting 45% renewable electricity by 2030, driven by the energy crisis.

2023 — 500 GW milestone

Global new renewable additions exceed 500 GW in a single year for the first time (IEA, Renewables 2023).

2030 Target

SDG 13 aims to substantially increase renewable share globally and double energy efficiency improvements.

Key Facts

Despite record additions, the IEA warns that the pace of renewable deployment must triple by 2030 to stay on track for net-zero emissions by 2050. Policy ambition must match the urgency of the science.

What Students Can Do

The science is clear — and so is the opportunity. Rapid, coordinated action across all sectors of society can still limit the worst impacts of the energy crisis. Students and young people have a vital role to play.

Individual Actions with High Impact

  • Switch to a green energy tariff: If you pay energy bills, choose a supplier sourcing 100% electricity from renewables — removing thousands of kg of CO² annually.
  • Reduce energy consumption: Turn off lights and standby devices; enable energy-saving modes on all electronics.
  • Advocate for campus solar: Join or start a sustainability society pushing your university to install rooftop solar PV — as the University of Edinburgh did in 2022.
  • Use public transport or cycle: Transport is the hardest sector to decarbonise; every car trip avoided matters.
  • Study and work in green sectors: Every profession needs climate-literate people — from engineering and policy to law, finance and healthcare.
  • Use your voice: Vote, campaign, write to representatives, and support organisations working on clean energy policy and community energy co-operatives.

Key Facts

Transitioning to a green economy could create 24 million new jobs globally by 2030. Solar and wind energy are now the cheapest sources of new electricity generation in most of the world — meaning clean energy is no longer a trade-off with affordability.

The Road Ahead

The IEA projects that for the world to reach net-zero emissions by 2050, 90% of global electricity must come from renewables. This will require an estimated $4 trillion per year in clean energy investment — but the rewards in health, jobs and climate stability are immense.

Fig 5. The cities of tomorrow will be powered by distributed, clean renewable energy — the transition is already well underway.

Emerging Technologies to Watch

  • Green Hydrogen: Made using renewable electricity and electrolysis — can store energy seasonally and decarbonise heavy industry and shipping.
  • Floating Solar (Floatovoltaics): PV panels on reservoirs and lakes that also reduce water evaporation.
  • Agrivoltaics: Combining solar panels with food production on the same land, benefiting both energy output and farmers.
  • Next-generation batteries: Solid-state, flow batteries and gravity storage enabling multi-day grid backup.
  • Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS): Drilling deeper to access geothermal energy anywhere on Earth, not just volcanic regions.

Key Facts

Renewable energy is no longer an alternative — it is the fastest-growing and cheapest electricity technology available. The question is no longer whether we can power the world on renewables, but whether we will move fast enough to matter.
